Reading the Frame: What a Screenshot Knows That the Source Does Not

An earlier pass rebuilt the Sunflower Cybercity scene against its reference artwork, component by component. Flower geometry, window textures, the sun's gradient, the laser lattice: each one was examined, found wanting, fixed, and verified. Every one of those fixes was correct.

This pass started by ignoring all of it. Render the live scene headlessly, put the capture next to the artwork, and do not open a single source file until the two pictures have been compared.

That inversion is the whole point of this post, because the two methods do not find the same class of bug. Checking a component against a reference asks "is this piece right?" Checking a frame against a reference asks "is the picture right?" A scene can pass every instance of the first question and still fail the second, and this one did.

Eight Things the Picture Said

The sky had a carefully built gradient running from deep navy at the zenith down to a teal horizon band. It had never been visible. The horizon colour was placed at the very bottom of a screen-space texture, and the bottom of that texture is below the ground plane, so the entire gradient was rendering underneath the world where nothing could ever see it. In the frame, the sky was simply dark.

The sun was a featureless white blob. Its shader defined a three-stop ramp and a set of horizontal band cuts, all correct, all invisible: the whole disc sat above the bloom threshold, so the post-processing pass clipped every one of those values to white together.

The car was parked directly over the sun. It spawned about ten units from the lens, and because a centred mouse pointer mapped to the middle of its altitude range rather than the bottom, it floated at mid-height. The reference has a car on a road. This had a silhouette across the sky.

The sunflower field, a field being half the name of the place, read as a single row of flowers along the horizon. Two and a half thousand instances had been spread across a hundred and forty units of half-width, which is not enough density to look like anything at any distance.

The oversized foreground blooms were not there. Not badly positioned, not too dark: absent. They sat about sixty-six degrees off the camera axis against a horizontal half-angle of roughly forty-two degrees. They had been rendering off-screen since the day they were added, and no amount of reading their code would have said so, because their code is fine.

The mountains were lost behind fog tuned to a range that dissolved them before the camera reached them. The laser beams were built as cylinders, which fall below one pixel of width at two hundred units and vanish. And the skyline had no depth, because tower height had been tied to road proximity, which meant the tallest towers were raised hardest against the one corridor that needed to stay open.

The Fixes Were Mostly Framing

Very little of this was fixed by writing better shaders. The horizon moved to where a screen-space background actually puts the horizon. The fog was retinted to that same teal so distance dissolves into sky instead of into a purple band. The car moved down the road and its altitude range was rebased so a resting pointer hovers it just above the deck.

The city's inner edge became an angular corridor rather than a fixed offset, so the clear wedge of sky holds its width all the way to the vanishing point instead of closing over it, and tower height was decoupled from road proximity entirely.

Three things were added, all of them present in the artwork and previously missing: a tiled procedural sunflower carpet, the elevated monorail guideways that run down each side of the frame, and the four-pointed star flares in the upper sky. The monorail turned out to matter more than expected. Two hard converging lines above the skyline are the strongest depth cue in the whole picture.

The Carpet Made It Cheaper

The most useful result of the pass is slightly counterintuitive. The field now reads as dense all the way to the horizon, and it does so with fewer instanced flowers than before, not more.

The obvious move when a field looks sparse is to raise the instance count, and that was tried: four thousand two hundred. It was slow and it still did not read correctly, because the problem was never the count. It was that the budget was being spent uniformly across a distance range where most of those flowers land smaller than a single pixel. Geometry at that scale costs full price and returns nothing.

Handing the far field to a tiled texture and re-concentrating the instances into the near band, where individual blooms are actually resolvable, gave a denser-looking field at two thousand eight hundred instances. Spending less by spending it in the right place.

Five Bugs That Were Not the Assignment

Work like this turns over rocks. Instanced building batches were being frustum-culled against an unscaled unit box at the origin rather than against the space the batch actually occupies, which meant a batch spanning the entire skyline could disappear the moment the origin left the view. It had only ever worked because the camera framing happened to keep the origin on screen.

The road texture's repeat count was fractional, which broke the dashed centre line where it wrapped. The screenshot tool's canvas capture had been failing on every single run, because it waited for a canvas driven by requestAnimationFrame to hold still, and such a canvas never does. The same tool leaked its development server on teardown, signalling the package-manager wrapper instead of the server process, leaving it holding a port and a core after every run.

The fifth came from a deliberate read of the build and publishing workflows before proposing any of this for merge. A sibling script pruned its own output retention window without filtering to directories, and because the throwaway captures the first script leaves behind sort after the dated folder names, the keep-most-recent window was protecting the junk while the prune deleted the real evaluation records.

That same workflow read caught something else worth naming, because it is a mistake that is easy to make and invisible until someone measures. The regenerated preview image had grown to 1.72 MB as a PNG, on an asset loaded by both the homepage and a blog post. A shaded 3D render is nothing but gradients and dither, which is the worst possible case for lossless compression. The same frame as a JPEG is 268 KB and looks identical.

The Method, Not the Scene

The scene is better, but the transferable part is the ordering. Verifying a system component by component is necessary and it is not sufficient, because composition errors live in the relationships between correct parts and are invisible from inside any one of them. The sky gradient was correct. The camera was correct. The fact that one rendered underneath the other was a property of neither.

An artifact you can look at is the cheapest possible check on that class of error, and it is worth generating one before reading any code, precisely so that what you see is not filtered through what you already believe the code does. The foreground flowers are the clearest case. Their geometry is good, their materials are good, their placement code is readable and does exactly what it says. They were off-screen for months. Nothing but a picture was ever going to say so.
